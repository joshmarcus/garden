## Reaction

I want Garden to take written work far enough that my next meaningful involvement is a decision about the product. Adding a colleague should divide responsibility, not create a second person who must watch the same machinery. Phase 10 moves toward that outcome. I would tell a colleague: Garden turns our written plans into reviewed pull requests, with each person's automation responsible for their assigned work. That is useful enough for me to keep investing in it.

My assessment comes from reading the supplied phase evidence, author and reviewer finals, documentation, and released source. I tried to open both supplied walkthrough directories and received permission errors, so I cannot describe their Inbox, Board, task, or run pages as personal observations. The phase notes also explicitly identify the captured operator build as older than release 0.4.3. I did not run the app or checks during this review. I give the reported passing release CI and isolated installation exercises credit without treating them as a production multiplayer session.

The ownership work matters more to me than the word multiplayer. I want to assign a phase once and have its tasks follow that assignment unless I deliberately make an exception. CG-687 and CG-690 address that expectation, including explicit unassignment. Automatic binding for an admitted OS user also removes a needless daily chore. I accept administrative setup; I do not want to identify myself again whenever I open my own local tool. Keeping project browsing separate from execution assignment is equally valuable. Looking at a colleague's project must not volunteer my workers to run it.

The handoff evidence is where I became more willing to leave Garden running. A disconnected machine is an awkward problem, but it is much less frightening than two machines both believing they own the same work. The CG-631 finals describe concrete fixes preventing unresolved work from being erased or detached from its original claim. CG-633 then addresses cancellation that had not actually been confirmed. Those are precisely the surprises that would make me stop using an autonomous tool. I accept waiting for a worker to stop, or for an uncertain external operation to be resolved, before another person takes over. My impatience is not sufficient evidence that the old worker is gone.

I also accept the owner's Git choice. I already need a shared repository; maintaining another authoritative service would add an operational responsibility I did not come here to acquire. The tradeoff is clear in the delivered guide: when the remote is unavailable, new shared work waits. That is reasonable for my small team. The separately published viewer projection is another useful boundary. I can share selected progress without treating my private working environment as a public dashboard, while understanding that publication cannot retract information someone already copied.

The reported interaction changes target the right daily irritations. My work should appear without typing an internal owner identifier. A task's purpose should precede routine diagnostics. An attention count should mean I can make a decision, not that automation is waiting somewhere. CG-691's shared actionable count and CG-692's sidebar project selection support those expectations. I am crediting their implementation and reported checks, not claiming the current layout passed my own visual inspection. Mark done is a practical addition for work completed elsewhere, provided its recorded reason and distinction from merged acceptance remain visible.

Economy, Default, and Fast are also easier choices when they change concurrency while retaining my configured models and caps. I would not interpret Economy as a promise to halve the total bill. What would concern me more is paying repeatedly to recover the same delivery. The inventory records 18 lifetime revisions for CG-633 and 14 for CG-631. Reading the actual finals changed how I interpret those counts: some rounds closed serious ownership defects; another restored protection lost during a rebase; CG-690 corrected an invalid expectation that a browser identity could change a server's execution authority. I want that protection retained. I also welcome CG-657's reduction of procedural send-backs. Counts alone cannot tell me which expense was avoidable.

Two remaining chores weaken the experience. Following the multiplayer documentation takes me between a coordinator-based specification and a Git-based guide, and the guide does not supply the setup and recovery procedure the specification promises. That leaves me deciding which instructions to trust before involving a colleague. Separately, the owner had to reopen this already released phase merely to request reflection. I should be able to learn from finished work without making its status misleading. I would put those corrections into the frozen next phase as proposals, close this retrospective normally, and preserve the separate decision about production activation.
